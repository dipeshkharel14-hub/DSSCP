Module 1: Vaccines & Immunology (Applied Biology Unit)

1. Fundamental Definitions & Core Concepts

Vaccine: A biological preparation containing antigenic proteins, weakened (attenuated) live pathogens, or inactivated dead microorganisms that is administered orally or injected into the body to stimulate host immune memory and induce specific antibody production.

Vaccination: The administrative process of introducing live attenuated, killed, or recombinant antigenic substances into a human or animal body to induce active immune protection against infectious diseases.

Immunization: The complete physiological process through which a host develops immunity and long-term resistance against a specific pathogen following natural infection or vaccine administration.

Historical Milestones in Immunology:
• Edward Jenner (1796): Observed that milkmaids infected with cowpox were immune to smallpox. He inoculated young James Phipps with cowpox fluid and successfully protected him against smallpox, laying the foundation of modern vaccination.
• Louis Pasteur (July 1885): Coined the term Vaccine (derived from Latin vacca meaning cow, in honor of Jenner). Pasteur developed the rabies vaccine (first successfully tested on Joseph Meister) and the anthrax vaccine, establishing the concept of pathogenic attenuation.

2. Antigens vs Antibodies

Antigens (Immunogens): Foreign macromolecular substances (typically proteins, polysaccharides, or glycoproteins) that enter host tissues and trigger a specific immune response by binding to specific host antibody receptors (paratopes) via their antigenic determinant sites (epitopes).

Antibodies (Immunoglobulins - Ig): Y-shaped defensive proteins synthesized by differentiated B-lymphocytes (plasma cells) in response to foreign antigen recognition. The five primary classes of human immunoglobulins are IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, and IgE.

Criteria Antigen Antibody
Origin Exogenous or foreign molecules originating outside host tissues. Endogenous defensive proteins synthesized internally by host B-cells.
Chemical Nature Proteins, polysaccharides, lipopolysaccharides, or nucleoproteins. Exclusively proteins (Immunoglobulins IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, IgE).
Binding Site Contains one or more Epitopes (antigenic determinants). Contains hypervariable Paratopes at the variable region (Fab).
Primary Function Triggers immune cell activation or causes cellular pathogenesis. Neutralizes pathogens, agglutinates microbes, and promotes opsonization.

3. Classification of Immunity

Immunity represents the overall biological capability of an organism to recognize, neutralize, and destroy invading foreign pathogens or toxic substances.

A. Natural (Innate / Non-Specific) Immunity

Inborn genetic resistance present from birth. It operates via physical barriers (skin, mucous membranes), chemical barriers (stomach HCl, lysozyme in tears), cellular barriers (neutrophils, macrophages), and cytokine barriers (interferons). In newborns, maternal antibodies (IgG crossing the placenta and IgA via colostrum) provide crucial temporary innate humoral protection.

B. Acquired (Adaptive / Specific) Immunity

Immunity acquired during an individual's lifespan following contact with specific antigens. Characterized by specificity, memory, and self-nonself discrimination.

  • Active Immunity: Produced when the host's own immune system is exposed to foreign antigens (via natural infection or vaccination) and actively manufactures its own antibodies and memory B/T lymphocytes.
    • Natural Active Immunity: Direct recovery from an infection (e.g., lifelong immunity after smallpox or chickenpox).
    • Artificial Active Immunity: Induced through vaccines (e.g., BCG, Sabin OPV, MMR, Hepatitis B).
  • Passive Immunity: Developed by directly transferring pre-formed readymade antibodies into an individual. Provides immediate, short-term protection without forming memory cells.
    • Natural Passive Immunity: Transfer of maternal IgG across placenta or maternal IgA through milk/colostrum.
    • Artificial Passive Immunity: Injection of hyperimmune serum, such as Antivenin (against snake venom), Anti-Tetanus Serum (ATS), or Rabies Immunoglobulin (RIG).

4. Four Major Categories of Vaccines

  1. Killed / Inactivated Vaccines: Formulated by killing pathogenic bacteria or viruses using heat, radiation, or chemical agents like formaldehyde (formalin) while keeping structural antigen surface proteins intact. They are safe and stable but require booster doses.
    Examples: Salk Injectable Polio Vaccine (IPV), Rabies Vaccine, Cholera Vaccine, Inactivated Typhoid Vaccine.
  2. Live Attenuated Vaccines: Created by weakening (attenuating) live pathogens through repeated subculturing in abnormal laboratory media or chemical treatment. The organism remains alive and replicates slowly to produce a strong, long-lasting cellular and humoral immune response without causing full-scale disease.
    Examples: BCG (Bacillus Calmette–Guérin against Tuberculosis), Sabin Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV), MMR Vaccine (Measles, Mumps, Rubella), Yellow Fever Vaccine.
  3. Toxoid Vaccines: Produced by treating bacterial exotoxins with dilute formalin to neutralize their clinical toxicity while preserving immunogenicity. They induce antitoxin antibodies.
    Examples: Tetanus Toxoid (TT), Diphtheria Toxoid.
  4. Recombinant / Subunit Vaccines: Developed using recombinant DNA technology. The gene encoding a specific surface antigen is isolated, inserted into an expression vector, and expressed inside host production cells like yeast cells (Saccharomyces cerevisiae).
    Examples: Recombinant Hepatitis B Vaccine (expressing HBsAg in yeast), Human Papillomavirus (HPV) Vaccine.

Module 2: Transgenic Animals & Biopharming

1. Definition & Landmark Discovery

Transgenic Animals: Animals whose genome has been stably modified by the artificial integration of a foreign functional gene (transgene) originating from another individual or species using recombinant DNA techniques.

Historic Breakthrough (December 1982):
Richard Palmiter and Ralph Brinster created the world's first transgenic "super mouse" by fusing the human growth hormone (hGH) gene with the promoter region of the mouse metallothionein-1 (MT-1) gene. The resulting transgenic mice expressed elevated growth hormone levels and grew to almost double the normal size of control mice.

2. Methods for Gene Transfer into Animal Embryos

  • DNA Microinjection Method: Direct physical injection of purified foreign DNA solution directly into the male pronucleus of a newly fertilized single-cell zygote using a micro-glass needle (0.5–1.5 µm tip) under microscopic control. The injected zygote is then surgically implanted into a pseudopregnant foster mother.
  • Retroviral Vector-Mediated Transfer: Recombinant retroviruses rendered replication-defective are engineered to carry the desired transgene. Early-stage embryos (blastomeres during cleavage) are infected with the viral vector, which integrates the target genetic material into host genomic DNA.
  • Embryonic Stem (ES) Cell Method: Target foreign genes are introduced into cultured pluripotent embryonic stem cells derived from blastocysts in vitro via electroporation or homologous recombination. Genetically transformed ES cells are screened, selected, and microinjected into recipient blastocysts to produce chimeric transgenic offspring.

3. Major Applications & Key Transgenic Examples

Transgenic Organism Genetic Modification Made Primary Application & Benefit
Rosie Cow (1997) Human α-lactalbumin gene inserted into bovine genome. Produces humanized milk containing 2.4 g/L of human α-lactalbumin protein, providing a nutritionally complete alternative for human infants.
Transgenic Sheep & Goats Human genes encoding therapeutic blood proteins inserted. Biopharming: Secretes human blood clotting Factor IX (for hemophilia) and α-1-antitrypsin (AAT, for emphysema) directly in milk.
Transgenic Pigs Disruption (knockout) of galactosyltransferase genes. Serves as xenograft organ donors (kidneys, heart valves) for human transplantation with minimal risk of hyperacute immune rejection.
Transgenic Fish (Salmon, Carp) Chimeric growth hormone (GH) genes inserted. Attains double the normal body mass (2× size) and dramatically accelerated growth rates for commercial aquaculture.
Transgenic Mice Oncogenes or viral receptors integrated into genome. Serves as disease models for studying human cancer, Alzheimer's disease, and testing vaccine safety (e.g., polio vaccine safety testing).

Module 3: Organ & Tissue Transplantation

1. Definition & Classification of Grafts

Transplantation: The surgical replacement or transfer of viable cells, tissues, or entire functional organs from a donor to a recipient host, or from one anatomical location to another within the same individual, to restore damaged physiological functions.

Graft Category Donor & Recipient Relationship Genetic Homology Graft Rejection Risk
Autograft Graft transferred within the same individual (e.g., skin graft from thigh to facial burn area, vein bypass). 100% Identical Zero Rejection
Isograft (Syngeneic) Graft transferred between genetically identical twins (monozygotic twins). 100% Identical Zero Rejection
Allograft (Homograft) Graft transferred between genetically non-identical members of the same species (e.g., human-to-human kidney/heart transplant). Genetically Distinct High Risk (T-cell CMI)
Xenograft (Heterograft) Graft transferred between individuals belonging to different species (e.g., pig heart valve or liver to human). Species Divergent Hyperacute Rejection

2. Mechanism of Graft Rejection & Immunosuppression

Graft Rejection Mechanism: Driven primarily by Cell-Mediated Immunity (CMI). The host's immune system recognizes foreign Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) or Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) markers present on donor organ cells. Cytotoxic T-lymphocytes (CD8+ T-cells) and Helper T-cells (CD4+ T-cells) infiltrate the graft, releasing cytokines, inducing vascular inflammation, thrombotic occlusion, and tissue necrosis.

Immunosuppressive Therapy: To prevent allograft rejection, recipients are administered immunosuppressive drugs:

  • Cyclosporine A: Lipophilic peptide extracted from the soil fungus Tolypocladium inflatum. It selectively inhibits calcineurin pathways, blocking interleukin-2 (IL-2) transcription and preventing T-cell activation.
  • Other drugs include Tacrolimus (FK506), Corticosteroids, and Azathioprine.

3. Corneal Grafting (Keratoplasty) & Immunological Privilege

Corneal transplants represent the most successful organ grafting procedure in human medicine, achieving over 90% success rates without needing aggressive immunosuppressant drugs.

Why Corneal Transplants do not undergo Graft Rejection:
The cornea is an immunologically privileged site due to the following structural and immune features:
1. Avascularity: The cornea is entirely devoid of blood vessels and lymphatic vessels. Host cytotoxic T-lymphocytes (CD8+ T-cells) and antibodies cannot travel through blood circulation to infiltrate or attack the donor graft.
2. Blood-Aqueous Barrier: Prevents immune cell migration into the anterior chamber.
3. Immunosuppressive Microenvironment: Soluble factors like TGF-β in aqueous humor activate immune tolerance mechanisms.
